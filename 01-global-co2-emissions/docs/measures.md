# DAX measures

Every measure in this report, with its definition and the reasoning behind it.
Forty in total, plus one generated by the what-if parameter, all held in a
dedicated `_Measures` table so the calculation layer sits in one place.

## Why most of the source columns were deleted

The source file has 79 columns and the model keeps 22. What was dropped falls
into three groups, and all three are dropped for the same reason.

`co2_per_capita`, `coal_co2_per_capita` and the rest are ratios. Averaging
Qatar's 35.6 tonnes per person and China's 8.0 gives 21.8, which describes
nobody. The correct figure for the two together is total emissions over total
population.

`share_global_co2` and the other `share_global_*` columns are also ratios, and
the same argument applies. Germany's 1.8% and China's 30.9% do not average to
16.4% of anything.

`co2_growth_abs` and `co2_growth_prct` are period comparisons that only make
sense at one grain, and a model that can slice by year should compute them
rather than store them.

Each of these comes back below as a measure, computed from the additive columns
that remain. The rule: **if summing it across rows produces nonsense, it does
not belong in a table.**

## Base quantities

These are the only things in the model that can be legitimately summed.

### CO2

```dax
CO2 = SUM(Emissions[co2])
```

Annual production emissions in million tonnes: emissions released inside a
country's borders. Everything else is built on this.

### Population, GDP

```dax
Population = SUM(Emissions[population])
```

```dax
GDP = SUM(Emissions[gdp])
```

`gdp` is in international dollars at purchasing power parity, not market
exchange rates. In 2018 that puts China at 18.15 trillion and the United States
at 18.14, which is only true on a PPP basis. Every intensity figure in the
report inherits that choice.

### The fuel breakdown

```dax
Coal CO2 = SUM(Emissions[coal_co2])
Oil CO2 = SUM(Emissions[oil_co2])
Gas CO2 = SUM(Emissions[gas_co2])
Cement CO2 = SUM(Emissions[cement_co2])
Flaring CO2 = SUM(Emissions[flaring_co2])
Land Use CO2 = SUM(Emissions[land_use_change_co2])
```

On the World row these five plus `other_industry_co2` sum to exactly the `co2`
total, so a stacked chart of them reconciles with the headline figure and leaves
no residual.

### Consumption and trade

```dax
Consumption CO2 = SUM(Emissions[consumption_co2])
```

```dax
Trade CO2 = SUM(Emissions[trade_co2])
```

Emissions attributed to what a country consumes rather than what it produces,
and the net balance between them. Positive `Trade CO2` means a net importer of
embodied emissions.

Both exist only for 1990 to 2020 and for 120 countries, which constrains the
whole trade page.

### Cumulative CO2

```dax
Cumulative CO2 =
CALCULATE(SUM(Emissions[cumulative_co2]), Year[year] = MAX(Year[year]))
```

The first trap in this dataset. `cumulative_co2` is already a running total per
country-year, so summing it across years adds 1750's total to 1751's to 1752's
and produces a figure in the hundreds of millions.

Restricting to the latest year in context makes it correct at any filter and
correct with no year filter at all, where it returns the 2021 position.

## Reading the World row

The second trap, and the reason for a naming convention.

A share of global emissions needs a denominator, and the obvious
`CALCULATE([CO2], ALL(Country))` is badly wrong, because the fact table holds
countries **and** World **and** six continents **and** four income groups. That
denominator counts the planet five times over.

The denominator is a specific row, not an aggregate of rows.

```dax
Global CO2 =
CALCULATE(SUM(Emissions[co2]), ALL(Country), Country[country] = "World")
```

```dax
Global Cumulative CO2 =
CALCULATE(
    SUM(Emissions[cumulative_co2]),
    ALL(Country),
    Country[country] = "World",
    Year[year] = MAX(Year[year])
)
```

```dax
Global Coal CO2 = CALCULATE(SUM(Emissions[coal_co2]), ALL(Country), Country[country] = "World")
Global Oil CO2 = CALCULATE(SUM(Emissions[oil_co2]), ALL(Country), Country[country] = "World")
Global Gas CO2 = CALCULATE(SUM(Emissions[gas_co2]), ALL(Country), Country[country] = "World")
Global Cement CO2 = CALCULATE(SUM(Emissions[cement_co2]), ALL(Country), Country[country] = "World")
Global Flaring CO2 = CALCULATE(SUM(Emissions[flaring_co2]), ALL(Country), Country[country] = "World")
```

**The convention: a `Global` prefix means the measure reads the World row and
ignores the report's country filter. No prefix means it answers for whatever is
selected.**

The `ALL(Country)` is what makes that work, and it has to live inside the
measure. A visual-level filter cannot override a report-level one in Power BI:
the two combine with AND, so `entity_type = Country` and `entity_type = World`
together return nothing.

## Ratios

Every one of these replaces a column that was deleted.

### CO2 per Capita

```dax
CO2 per Capita = DIVIDE([CO2] * 1000000, [Population])
```

`co2` is in million tonnes and `population` is in people, so the factor converts
to tonnes per person. Checks against China 2021 at 8.05, matching the deleted
`co2_per_capita` column.

A ratio of sums, not an average of ratios, so it re-aggregates correctly at any
level. The world total comes out near 4.6 rather than the 21.8 an average of the
column would produce.

### CO2 per GDP

```dax
CO2 per GDP = DIVIDE([CO2] * 1000000000, [GDP])
```

Kilograms of CO2 per dollar of output. Checks against the United Kingdom 2018 at
0.149.

### Share of Global CO2, Share of Global Cumulative CO2

```dax
Share of Global CO2 = DIVIDE([CO2], [Global CO2])
```

```dax
Share of Global Cumulative CO2 = DIVIDE([Cumulative CO2], [Global Cumulative CO2])
```

China 2021 returns 30.9%, matching the deleted `share_global_co2` column, which
is the check that the World denominator is being read correctly.

Note that the 233 countries sum to 97.2% rather than 100%. The missing 2.8% is
International transport, which belongs to no country.

### Trade Balance %

```dax
Trade Balance % = DIVIDE([Trade CO2], [CO2])
```

The United Kingdom 2020 returns 42.4%: it imports emissions equal to 42% of
everything it produces. Singapore returns 379%.

## Responsibility

### Responsibility Ratio

```dax
Responsibility Ratio = DIVIDE([Share of Global CO2], [Share of Global Cumulative CO2])
```

A country's share of today's emissions divided by its share of all emissions
ever. Above 1 means it emits more now than its history would suggest; below 1,
less.

Nobody designed this to sort by date of industrialisation, and it does anyway.
United Kingdom 0.21, Germany 0.34, France 0.37, United States 0.56 at one end.
India 2.22, China 2.15, Saudi Arabia 1.88, Iran 1.79 at the other.

### Top 10 Share

```dax
Top 10 Share =
VAR Top10 =
    TOPN(
        10,
        CALCULATETABLE(VALUES(Country[country]), ALL(Country), Country[entity_type] = "Country"),
        [CO2],
        DESC
    )
RETURN
    DIVIDE(CALCULATE([CO2], Top10), [Global CO2])
```

68.3% for 2021. The `TOPN` is recalculated for whichever year is in context
rather than hard-coding today's list, so moving to 1950 gives that year's ten
largest emitters and their share.

The `CALCULATETABLE` with `ALL(Country)` and an `entity_type` filter matters:
without it, `TOPN` would rank World first and return a share above 100%.

## Trade

```dax
CO2 in 1990 = CALCULATE([CO2], ALL(Year), Year[year] = 1990)
Consumption CO2 in 1990 = CALCULATE([Consumption CO2], ALL(Year), Year[year] = 1990)
```

```dax
CO2 Change since 1990 = DIVIDE([CO2], [CO2 in 1990]) - 1
Consumption Change since 1990 = DIVIDE([Consumption CO2], [Consumption CO2 in 1990]) - 1
```

The pair that carries the trade page. The United States returns -7.9% on
production and +3.0% on consumption: it cut what it made and increased what it
bought.

The `ALL(Year)` lets them reach back to 1990 while the visual sits on 2020.

### Trade Line Title

```dax
Trade Line Title =
SELECTEDVALUE(Country[country], "All countries selected")
    & ": emissions produced against emissions consumed"
```

Used as a dynamic title through the title's conditional formatting, so a chart
driven by a slicer describes what it is actually showing rather than what it
showed when the title was typed.

## Decoupling

These are driven by a what-if parameter, `Baseline Year`, a numeric range from
1990 to 2010. Power BI generates `Baseline Year Value` alongside it, which is
`SELECTEDVALUE('Baseline Year'[Baseline Year], 1990)`.

### The baseline pair

```dax
CO2 at Baseline =
VAR BaseYear = [Baseline Year Value]
RETURN CALCULATE([CO2], FILTER(ALL(Year), Year[year] = BaseYear))
```

```dax
GDP at Baseline =
VAR BaseYear = [Baseline Year Value]
RETURN CALCULATE([GDP], FILTER(ALL(Year), Year[year] = BaseYear))
```

The `VAR` is not decoration. `CALCULATE`'s shorthand boolean filter cannot
contain a measure, because the engine resolves the condition into a table before
evaluating anything and a measure's value is not known at that point. Writing
`Year[year] = [Baseline Year Value]` directly returns:

```
A function 'PLACEHOLDER' has been used in a True/False expression
that is used as a table filter expression. This is not allowed.
```

Evaluating the measure into a variable first, then filtering against the
variable, resolves it.

### The fixed endpoints

```dax
CO2 at 2018 = CALCULATE([CO2], ALL(Year), Year[year] = 2018)
GDP at 2018 = CALCULATE([GDP], ALL(Year), Year[year] = 2018)
CO2 at 1998 = CALCULATE([CO2], ALL(Year), Year[year] = 1998)
```

2018 is hard-coded because the GDP series stops there, so the endpoint is forced
by the data rather than chosen. Naming the year inside the measure means the
whole page needs no year filter, and nobody has to work out later why one page
shows 2018 while the rest of the report shows 2021.

### The comparison

```dax
CO2 Change from Baseline = DIVIDE([CO2 at 2018], [CO2 at Baseline]) - 1
GDP Change from Baseline = DIVIDE([GDP at 2018], [GDP at Baseline]) - 1
```

### Share of Fall by 1998

```dax
Share of Fall by 1998 =
VAR TotalFall = [CO2 at Baseline] - [CO2 at 2018]
VAR EarlyFall = [CO2 at Baseline] - [CO2 at 1998]
RETURN IF(TotalFall > 0, DIVIDE(EarlyFall, TotalFall))
```

The measure the decoupling page exists for. How much of a country's decline had
already happened by 1998.

Above 100% means emissions had fallen below their 2018 level by 1998 and then
partly recovered, which is what an economy collapsing looks like: Russia 128%,
Azerbaijan 117%, Estonia 105%, Belarus 104%.

Negative means emissions rose before they fell, which a transition collapse
cannot produce: Japan -320%, the Netherlands -430%, France -29%.

It catches early collapses and not late ones. Venezuela returns -235% because
its emissions rose until 2014 and then fell with its economy. One measure cannot
separate every case and this one does not claim to.

### Decoupled Countries

```dax
Decoupled Countries =
COUNTROWS(
    FILTER(
        VALUES(Country[country]),
        [GDP Change from Baseline] > 0
            && [CO2 Change from Baseline] < 0
            && [CO2 at Baseline] >= 20
    )
)
```

27 at a 1990 baseline, 27 at 2000, 32 at 2010.

The count rising with a later baseline was not expected. Countries leave the
list as the post-Soviet collapses fall outside the window, and others join as
they turn. Part of the rise is also mechanical: over eight years emissions have
less room to grow than over twenty-eight, so more countries land on the falling
side.

The count is therefore a weak indicator and the composition is the finding,
which is why the table beside it lists the countries by name.

The 20 Mt floor keeps out countries whose entire emissions are a rounding error.

## Utility

```dax
Countries with Data =
CALCULATE(DISTINCTCOUNT(Emissions[country]), NOT ISBLANK(Emissions[co2]))
```

219 for 2021. A plain `DISTINCTCOUNT` would count every entity in filter context
including those carrying no emissions figure for the selected year, so an early
year would claim 200 countries when 20 have data.
